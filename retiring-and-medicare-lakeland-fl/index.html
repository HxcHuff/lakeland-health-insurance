Step 1: Figure out which window you are in

Turning 65 as you retire: Initial Enrollment Period (IEP)

Medicare.gov describes the Initial Enrollment Period as seven months long: the three months before your 65th birthday month, your birthday month, and the three months after. Signing up before your birthday month generally means coverage starts the first day of your birthday month; dates after that month can delay the Part B start date. Use the official calendar on Medicare.gov for your birthday month.

Already 65+ with employer coverage: Special Enrollment Period (SEP)

Group health coverage based on current employment—yours or your spouse’s—may let you delay Part B without a late enrollment penalty while that coverage continues. When the job or the coverage ends, whichever comes first, Medicare.gov and Social Security describe an eight-month Special Enrollment Period to enroll in Part B. Confirm whether your employer plan qualifies before you delay.

Callout: COBRA and retiree coverage do not count as current employment coverage for this Part B window. Medicare.gov states that COBRA does not extend the limited time to sign up for Medicare. Using COBRA or retiree coverage to delay Part B can trigger a coverage gap and the lifetime Part B late enrollment penalty described on Medicare.gov.

If you started Social Security at least four months before you turn 65, Social Security notes that Medicare Part A and Part B usually start automatically. If you are not taking Social Security yet, you may need to sign up for Medicare yourself during your IEP or SEP.

Step 2: Time Part B with your employer plan’s end date

  1. Get the exact end date of your employer health coverage from HR in writing—not just your last day of work if coverage runs longer.
  2. Pick a Part B start date of the first of the month after that coverage ends, then confirm the effective date rules on SSA.gov (Part B only).
  3. When you are leaving a job after 65 and already have Part A, apply online at SSA.gov and submit CMS-40B with CMS-L564 (employment information your employer completes), as described on Social Security.

Part A: most people qualify for Part A with no premium based on work history; confirm on Medicare.gov costs. If you have a Health Savings Account, Medicare.gov says you and your employer should stop HSA contributions before Medicare starts, because Part A can be backdated up to six months when you apply. Ask a tax professional about HSA rules—an insurance agent is not a tax advisor.

Part B premiums change each year. CMS has not announced 2027 amounts at the time this page was reviewed; use Medicare.gov costs for current official figures rather than blog estimates.

Step 3: Choose how you will get your coverage

Original Medicare + Medigap + Part D

Original Medicare (Parts A and B) lets you use any provider nationwide that accepts Medicare, which can help if you travel. Medicare.gov describes Medigap open enrollment as a one-time six-month window that starts the month you are 65 or older and enrolled in Part B. During that window, insurers generally cannot use medical underwriting to deny a Medigap policy. Medigap does not include drug coverage; add a stand-alone Part D plan unless you have other creditable drug coverage.

Medicare Advantage (Part C)

Medicare Advantage plans are offered by Medicare-approved private insurers and often include Part D. Plans use provider networks. In Polk County, check whether each doctor and hospital location you use participates in a plan's network for the plan year you are comparing. Network participation is plan-specific; a health-system or facility name is not a guarantee.

City of Lakeland retirees: read this first

City retiree health benefits and spouse-premium rules can change your timing. Medicare.gov explains that when you have both Medicare and retiree coverage from a former employer, Medicare generally pays first and retiree coverage may pay second; if retiree coverage is the secondary payer, you may need Part A and Part B for the plan to pay correctly. Ask City HR whether your retiree coverage requires Medicare enrollment at 65 and how the plan coordinates with Parts A and B before you delay anything.

For spouse-premium and Medigap timing questions specific to city retirees, read City of Lakeland retiree Medicare and spouse premium.

If your spouse is under 65

Medicare is individual coverage. A spouse who is not yet Medicare-eligible needs separate health insurance until they turn 65—often COBRA, a Marketplace plan, or other private coverage depending on the household’s facts. Medicare.gov’s retiree and COBRA pages describe how those paths differ from Medicare enrollment timing for the Medicare-eligible spouse.

Households retiring before the younger spouse turns 65 can start with retiring before 65 in Florida.

Frequently asked questions

I'm retiring. When do I sign up for Medicare?

If you are turning 65 as you retire, Medicare.gov describes a seven-month Initial Enrollment Period: the three months before your 65th birthday month, your birthday month, and the three months after. If you are already 65 or older and leaving group health coverage based on current employment, you generally have eight months after employment or that coverage ends—whichever is first—to enroll in Part B without a late penalty. Confirm your window with Medicare.gov and Social Security.

I had employer coverage past 65. Can I sign up for Part B without a late penalty when I retire?

Yes, when the coverage was group health plan coverage based on current employment. Medicare.gov and Social Security describe an eight-month Special Enrollment Period to sign up for Part B without a late enrollment penalty after you stop working or lose that insurance, whichever happens first. COBRA and retiree coverage do not count as current employment for this window.

When should Part B start so I have no gap between my employer plan and Medicare?

Get your employer coverage end date from HR in writing, then choose a Part B start date that begins the first of the month after that coverage ends. Social Security assigns effective dates based on when you enroll; confirm the matching date on SSA.gov before you cancel employer coverage.

Is COBRA or retiree coverage the same as current employment coverage for Medicare timing?

No. Medicare.gov states that COBRA is not group health plan coverage for the current-employment Part B Special Enrollment Period and that COBRA does not extend the limited time to sign up for Medicare. Retiree coverage is also distinct from active employer coverage. Using COBRA or retiree coverage to delay Part B can create a coverage gap and a lifetime Part B late enrollment penalty.

Do I need Part D if I pick Medigap?

Medicare Supplement (Medigap) policies sold today do not include prescription drug coverage. Medicare.gov says you can wait to join a Medicare drug plan without penalty only while you have creditable prescription drug coverage and do not go more than 63 days in a row without it. Most people who choose Original Medicare plus Medigap add a stand-alone Part D plan or confirm creditable coverage from another source.
