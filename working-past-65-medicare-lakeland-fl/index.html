Who this page is for (and who it isn’t)

This guide is for Lakeland, Polk County, and Florida residents around age 64 to 66 who are retiring at 65 or later, still working with job-based coverage, or covered on a spouse’s employer plan. The question is when to enroll in Medicare Parts A and B—and how drug coverage fits—without creating a late-enrollment penalty.

It is not the first page for leaving work before Medicare eligibility, shopping Marketplace coverage as your only plan, or comparing next year’s Medicare Advantage or Part D options after you are already enrolled. If you retire before 65, start with retiring before 65 in Florida. For plan-type comparisons after Parts A and B are in place, use the Medicare hub.

Retiring at 65 or later: when to sign up for Part B

Retirement and Medicare are two separate calendars. Medicare.gov ties the Part B Special Enrollment Period to the end of current-employer group coverage—not to your retirement party date and not to COBRA. When qualifying job-based coverage ends because you or your spouse stop working, you generally have eight months to sign up for Part B without a late enrollment penalty. That window starts when employment or the group plan ends, whichever happens first, even if you take COBRA or retiree coverage afterward.

Before the last day of work, ask HR in writing how the employer plan coordinates with Medicare, whether Part A or Part B is required for claims to pay correctly, and whether prescription drug coverage is creditable for Part D. Keep the last day of current-employer coverage and any creditable-coverage letter. If you already have Part A and need Part B after employment ends, Social Security may use forms CMS-40B and CMS-L564 (employer certification)—confirm the current forms and filing options on SSA.gov.

Part D is a separate clock: if you lose creditable drug coverage, Medicare.gov generally allows 63 days before a Part D late-enrollment penalty can apply. Medicare Open Enrollment in the fall is for people already in Medicare Advantage or Part D; it does not replace the retirement-related Part B window. A licensed Florida health agent can help you line up coverage type, dates, and local provider checks before you contact Social Security; that review is not enrollment.

Retiree coverage and COBRA: different from current-employer coverage

Retiree health plans and COBRA continuation coverage are common after a job ends, but Medicare.gov does not treat them as current-employment group coverage for the Part B Special Enrollment Period. Retiree insurance may not pay correctly if you do not have both Part A and Part B. The end of retiree coverage or COBRA is listed among situations that do not open a new Part B Special Enrollment Period—meaning you cannot wait until those plans end to start Medicare without penalty risk.

COBRA can still help with medical bills for a limited time, yet Medicare.gov says getting COBRA does not extend the eight-month Part B window that started when work or qualifying group coverage ended. Treat COBRA as a possible bridge, not as a way to pause Medicare enrollment. If you are not yet Medicare-eligible and comparing COBRA with Marketplace coverage, use the losing-coverage checklist.

City and large-employer retiree benefits in the Lakeland area— including plans discussed in our City of Lakeland retiree Medicare article—still require their own HR answers about Medicare coordination. Confirm how the retiree plan pays when you are Medicare-eligible before you delay Part B.

When your spouse is still working (or you are on a spouse’s plan)

Medicare.gov and Social Security use the same current-employment framing whether the insurance is from your job or your spouse’s job. If you are turning 65 while covered on a spouse’s active employer plan, you may be able to delay Part B without a late enrollment penalty until the spouse stops working or that group coverage ends—whichever happens first—then use the eight-month Special Enrollment Period.

That path is fact-specific. Ask the employer that sponsors the plan whether you must enroll in Part A or Part B for the plan to pay, whether the plan is large-group coverage as Medicare defines it, and whether your enrollment would affect dependents. Spouses who retire while the other partner still works may have one person on Medicare and one on employer coverage; each person’s Part B timing follows their own coverage type.

For household planning, pair this section with the turning-65 Florida checklist and, when retiree benefits are involved, the City of Lakeland retiree article linked above.

Still working past 65 with employer coverage

If you or your spouse still have group health insurance from a current job, Medicare.gov says you may be able to wait to sign up for Part B without a late enrollment penalty until you stop working or lose that insurance—if that happens first. Social Security also allows signing up while you remain working and covered by the group plan. Premium-free Part A can often be taken at 65 even when Part B is delayed; enrolling in any part of Medicare can affect Health Savings Account eligibility, so read the HSA section below before applying.

Still working is not enough by itself. Medicare.gov tells people to ask the employer whether they need Part A and Part B at 65 because job-based insurance might not cover services if they do not. Smaller-employer plans may coordinate differently with Medicare than large-group plans. Ask whether drug coverage is creditable for Part D and whether Medicare enrollment would change coverage for a spouse or dependent on the same policy.

Self-employed, Marketplace, and many private plans do not automatically qualify for the current-employer Part B delay. Medicare.gov says to ask whether the coverage is employer group health plan coverage as defined by the IRS; if it is not, sign up when first eligible, usually at 65, to avoid a Part B penalty. For coverage before Medicare eligibility, see self-employed health insurance.

Drug coverage: don’t create a Part D late-enrollment problem

Medical coverage and drug coverage are separate checks. Delaying Part B, when that delay is allowed, does not automatically protect you from a Part D late enrollment penalty.

Creditable coverage letters

Medicare.gov says that as long as you have creditable prescription drug coverage, you can wait to join a Medicare drug plan or a Medicare Advantage plan with drug coverage. You will not pay the Part D late enrollment penalty as long as you do not go more than 63 days without creditable drug coverage. If you are not sure the current drug plan is creditable, ask the plan. Medicare.gov says the plan has to tell you and will also send this information every year. Keep the letter. Do not send it to Medicare unless you are asked to document creditable coverage later.

Advantage with drug coverage vs standalone Part D later

After you have Part A and Part B, you can compare Original Medicare plus a standalone Part D plan with a Medicare Advantage plan that includes drug coverage. Those are later structural choices. They are not a substitute for the Part A and Part B enrollment decision, and they are not ranked here. When you are ready to compare paths, use Medicare Advantage vs Supplement and the provider and prescription check. Confirm formularies, pharmacies, and plan IDs for the exact year.

HSA and high-deductible plans: timing traps

Health Savings Account rules and Medicare entitlement can overlap in ways that create a tax problem if contributions continue too long. This section is educational only.

Stop-contribution timing themes per Medicare.gov

Medicare.gov’s working-past-65 guidance says that if you have an HSA, you and your employer should stop contributing to the HSA six months before you retire or apply for benefits from Social Security or the Railroad Retirement Board, to help avoid a tax penalty. That six-month theme is tied to how Medicare Part A can be retroactive when someone applies for Social Security or Medicare. Do not invent a different month count from this page. Confirm the current rule on Medicare.gov and with the people who administer the HSA.

Ask a qualified tax professional before you change contributions or file for Medicare if an HSA is involved.

Keeping a familiar doctor or hospital is not automatic on every Medicare path. Original Medicare, Medicare Advantage, and employer plans use different participation and billing rules.

Frequently asked questions

Can I delay Part B if I am still working?

It depends on whether the group coverage is based on current employment and how that coverage coordinates with Medicare. Medicare.gov says that if you or your spouse have health insurance from a job, you may be able to wait until you or your spouse stop working, or lose that insurance if that happens first, to sign up for Part B without a late enrollment penalty. Confirm the rules with Social Security and the employer benefits administrator before delaying Part B. COBRA and retiree coverage are not current-employment coverage for this Special Enrollment Period.

What if my spouse is the employee and I’m turning 65?

Medicare.gov and Social Security describe the same current-employment framing for coverage through your job or your spouse’s job. The Special Enrollment Period is typically available while qualifying group coverage based on current employment continues, and for eight months after employment or that coverage ends, whichever happens first. Confirm the individual facts with Social Security and the employer that provides the plan.

What if I’m self-employed at 65?

Confirm with Social Security and the current insurer. For coverage before Medicare eligibility, see self-employed health insurance.

Does AEP replace my Initial Enrollment Period decisions?

No. Medicare Open Enrollment generally runs October 15 through December 7. Medicare.gov describes it as the annual time when people with Medicare can make eligible Medicare Advantage or Part D changes, typically effective January 1 of the following year. That window does not replace the Initial Enrollment Period around age 65 or the Special Enrollment Period after current-employment coverage ends. Federal Medigap open enrollment generally lasts six months beginning when a person is both 65 or older and enrolled in Part B; it does not restart each fall. For later switching windows, see when you can switch Medicare plans in Florida.

I am retiring at 65—when should I sign up for Part B?

When current-employer group coverage ends because you stop working, Medicare.gov generally gives you eight months to enroll in Part B without a late penalty, starting when work or that coverage ends—whichever is first. COBRA and retiree coverage do not reset that clock. Confirm your last day of qualifying group coverage with HR and the matching rules with Social Security.

Does COBRA extend my time to enroll in Medicare Part B?

No. Medicare.gov states that COBRA is not group health plan coverage for the current-employment Part B Special Enrollment Period and that COBRA does not extend the limited time to sign up for Medicare. Sign up for Part B during the eight-month window that began when employment or qualifying group coverage ended, not when COBRA ends.

How is retiree coverage different from COBRA for Medicare timing?

Both are distinct from active employer coverage. Retiree plans may require Part A and Part B to pay claims, and the end of retiree coverage is not a listed qualifier for a new Part B Special Enrollment Period. COBRA is continuation coverage after a qualifying event; it may pay bills temporarily but does not pause the Part B enrollment clock. Ask HR how each plan coordinates with Medicare before you delay Part B.

Can I stay on my spouse’s employer plan after I retire?

Sometimes, if the employer allows dependents to remain on the active group plan and the plan still qualifies as current-employment coverage for you. Medicare timing then follows spouse-employer rules until that coverage ends. If you lose spouse coverage at retirement, your Part B window may start from that loss. Confirm dependent eligibility and Medicare coordination with the sponsoring employer.

What if I miss the Special Enrollment Period after retirement?

You may need to wait for the General Enrollment Period (January 1 through March 31 each year) and face a Part B late enrollment penalty that grows the longer you wait without qualifying coverage. Medicare.gov describes penalty rules on its avoid-penalties page. Gather coverage end dates and creditable drug coverage history before calling Social Security.

What to do next

Write down the coverage type, the last day of work, the last day of group coverage, and whether HR says Medicare is required for the plan to pay correctly. Keep the creditable-coverage letter and any HSA or high-deductible plan documents. Then compare the official Medicare.gov and Social Security instructions with a local review of doctors, prescriptions, and the enrollment window that actually applies.
