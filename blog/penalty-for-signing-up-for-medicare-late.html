What is the penalty for signing up for Medicare late?

Medicare late enrollment penalties are added to monthly premiums. They are not a one-time late fee. Part A, Part B, and Part D use different formulas. A Special Enrollment Period or creditable coverage can prevent a penalty if the official conditions are met.

By David Huff, licensed Florida health agent #W371813 | NPN 18213932

Last reviewed: September 29, 2026.

Direct answer: Medicare.gov says Part B late enrollment adds an extra 10 percent for each full 12-month period you could have signed up but did not. For most people that amount continues for as long as you have Part B. Part D adds 1 percent of the national base beneficiary premium for each month you went without creditable drug coverage, generally after a gap of 63 days or more. Part A has a separate rule for people who must buy Part A.

Where are these percentages published?

Use Medicare.gov: Avoid late enrollment penalties. That page states the Part B 10 percent per year rule, the Part D 1 percent per month rule, the 63-day creditable-coverage test, and the Part A 10 percent premium increase for people who must buy Part A, paid for twice the number of years they waited. This article does not repeat year-specific dollar premiums from the examples on that page because those amounts change.

When is there no Part B penalty?

Medicare.gov says you generally will not pay a Part B penalty if you qualify for a Special Enrollment Period or you enroll in a Medicare Savings Program. Current-employment group coverage is the common Special Enrollment Period. See signing up while still working. Marketplace coverage is not the same test. If you missed the Initial Enrollment Period and have no Special Enrollment Period, the General Enrollment Period is January 1 through March 31, coverage starts the month after you sign up, and a penalty may apply.

How is Part D different?

Part D looks at creditable prescription drug coverage. Medicare.gov says you generally will not pay a Part D penalty if you have creditable drug coverage or you qualify for Extra Help. If you go 63 days or more without Medicare drug coverage or other creditable coverage after you were eligible, a lifetime monthly penalty can apply after you join a drug plan. Ask your employer or union plan for its creditable-coverage notice each year and keep it.

Does Medigap have the same penalty?

No. Medigap does not use the Part B late enrollment percentage. Medigap has a federal six-month Open Enrollment Period starting when you are 65 or older and enrolled in Part B, plus other issue rights in some situations. Those rules are separate from Medicare Annual Enrollment. See Medicare plan-change windows and Medicare Advantage versus Medicare Supplement.

What should I do if I already have a penalty on my bill?

The premium notice from Medicare or the drug plan should show the penalty line. If you believe you had creditable coverage or a Special Enrollment Period, follow the reconsideration process described by the plan or by Social Security. Keep employer letters. Do not ignore the bill while you wait.

Joining a different Advantage or Part D plan does not erase a Part D late enrollment penalty. Medicare.gov says the monthly penalty continues after you switch plans. Part B penalties likewise continue while you have Part B.

If you are helping a parent, check whether they had employer coverage after 65 that nobody documented. Paperwork from a former employer can still matter. David Huff can help you organize the coverage history; he cannot remove a federal penalty.

Does living abroad or having VA coverage change this?

Some situations have special rules. Medicare.gov describes exceptions and creditable coverage in more detail than this article. If you had VA, TRICARE, or coverage based on current employment, bring those documents to Social Security rather than assuming a penalty applies or does not apply.

This page does not invent extra categories. If your facts are uncommon, use the Medicare.gov penalty page and Social Security. A licensed Florida health agent can help you assemble the coverage history.

Lakeland retirees who delayed Part B because a retiree plan felt complete are a common local example of why current-employment status has to be documented, not assumed.
