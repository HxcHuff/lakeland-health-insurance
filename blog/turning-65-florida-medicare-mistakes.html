Turning 65 in Florida? The 3 Medicare Mistakes That Cost Retirees Thousands Every Year

Every day, Floridians turning 65 make Medicare decisions that cost them thousands—and most of them are completely avoidable. Three mistakes account for the vast majority of financial damage.

Published April 6, 2026 | By David Huff, Licensed Insurance Agent

← Back to Blog

You're approaching 65, or maybe you just turned 65 in Florida. Congratulations on reaching this milestone. Now comes the hard part: navigating Medicare without accidentally making a decision that costs you thousands of dollars.

Here's the reality: Medicare is confusing by design. The government doesn't make it easy. Insurance companies profit from your confusion. And if you get it wrong, the financial penalties compound forever. This article breaks down the three most expensive mistakes we see—and more importantly, how to avoid them.

Mistake #1: Missing Your Initial Enrollment Period

Your Initial Enrollment Period (IEP) is a 7-month window: three months before your 65th birthday, your birthday month, and three months after. That's your chance to enroll in Medicare without facing permanent penalties.

Miss this window, and you'll pay late enrollment penalties for the rest of your life. We're not exaggerating. These penalties are forever, and they compound.

How the Penalties Work

Part B late enrollment penalty: 10% of the base premium for every 12-month period you were eligible but didn't enroll. This compounds year after year.

Part D late enrollment penalty: 1% of the national base beneficiary premium for each month without creditable drug coverage. Again, this is permanent.

Real Penalty Calculation Example

You turn 65 in March 2026 but don't enroll in Medicare Part B until January 2027—a 10-month delay. You've missed your IEP by 7 months. Your penalty: 10% × (7 months ÷ 12) = 5.83% of your base Part B premium, permanently added to your monthly cost. If your Part B premium is $185/month, that's an extra $10.80 per month forever. Over 15 years of retirement, that's $1,944 extra—just for missing your window by seven months.

Who Might Be Exempt

There are special circumstances where you might avoid penalties:

  • Still working and have employer coverage (but you must file for it correctly)
  • Spouse's employer coverage (similar rules apply)
  • Already on Social Security (you're automatically enrolled in Parts A & B)

The problem? These exceptions are technical and easy to mess up. If you think you qualify, work with a licensed agent to verify before your IEP ends.

Mistake #2: Choosing Medicare Advantage Without Understanding the Trade-offs

Medicare Advantage plans look fantastic on the marketing materials. No premium, dental coverage, vision, gym membership—it feels like you're winning the lottery. Then you try to see a specialist, and the plan denies it because they require "prior authorization."

Medicare Advantage isn't bad. But choosing it without understanding what you're giving up is a recipe for financial trouble.

The Real Differences

Here's what matters in actual healthcare situations:

Feature Medicare Advantage (Part C) Original Medicare + Supplement
Network Restrictions Yes—must use in-network doctors (except emergencies) No—see any doctor accepting Medicare
Prior Authorization Yes—plan pre-approves treatments No—you and your doctor decide
Out-of-Pocket Max Yes—plan covers after you hit the max No limit—supplement covers coinsurance
Travel Coverage Limited outside network area Full coverage nationwide
Specialist Referrals Required in most plans Not required
Dental/Vision/Hearing Often included Not included in Original Medicare
Prescription Drugs Usually included Need separate Part D plan

When Advantage Plans Shine

Medicare Advantage plans can be excellent if you:

  • Have a specific doctor or hospital you want to see that's in their network
  • Don't travel out of state frequently
  • Are willing to go through prior authorization for treatments
  • Want dental/vision/hearing covered (and use those benefits)

When Original Medicare + Supplement Wins

Original Medicare with a Medigap supplement is better if you:

  • Travel frequently or have a vacation home in another state
  • Have complex medical needs with multiple specialists
  • Want unlimited network access
  • Are willing to pay for a supplement to avoid network hassles

The Core Issue

Too many people choose Medicare Advantage because it looks free on paper, then get blindsided by network restrictions and prior authorization denials. You're not choosing between good and bad—you're choosing between different trade-offs. Know which trade-offs you can live with.

Mistake #3: Not Reviewing Your Plan Every Year

You picked a great Medicare plan in 2025. It had your doctors, the premium was reasonable, and your drug costs were manageable. Now it's 2026, and your insurance company completely changed the plan. Your favorite specialist is no longer in-network. Your drug formulary changed, and your medications cost twice as much. The premium jumped $50/month.

This happens constantly. And most people don't notice until they try to get care and find out their network changed.

What Changes Every Year

During Annual Enrollment Period (AEP) from October 15 to December 7 each year, plans can:

  • Change or remove providers from their network
  • Modify drug formularies (which drugs they cover and at what cost)
  • Adjust premiums and cost-sharing amounts
  • Change supplemental benefits (dental, vision, hearing coverage)
  • Modify prior authorization requirements

What doesn't change: You can't switch Medicare Advantage plans outside of AEP unless you have a qualifying life event. So if you discover in June that your plan is terrible, you're stuck until October 15.

The Solution

Every October, before the November 1st deadline to make changes effective January 1st, you should:

  1. Pull your current plan's provider network. Call your doctor and pharmacy to confirm they're still in-network for next year.
  2. Check your medications against the drug formulary. Call your pharmacy with your list of prescriptions.
  3. Review your plan's cost-sharing: deductibles, copays, coinsurance, out-of-pocket maximums.
  4. Compare your current plan against 1-2 alternatives that might save you money.
  5. Enroll in the new plan (or confirm your current one) before December 7.

Why This Matters

A Medigap supplement that wasn't right in 2025 might save you $2,000+ in 2026. A Medicare Advantage plan that was perfect might become useless if your network changes. The $15 of effort annually can save thousands. This is not optional for anyone serious about managing healthcare costs.
