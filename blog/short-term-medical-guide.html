Short-Term Medical Insurance in Florida: Uses and Limits

David Huff

Licensed Insurance Agent

Updated July 30, 2026

Short-term medical insurance can address a narrowly defined temporary coverage gap, but it is not a substitute for ACA-compliant major medical coverage. It may use medical underwriting, exclude pre-existing conditions, omit benefits required of ACA plans, and limit how much or how long it pays.

Important coverage notice

Short-term, limited-duration insurance is not subject to all federal protections that apply to comprehensive individual health insurance. Eligibility, covered services, exclusions, benefit limits, provider access, and effective dates depend on the specific policy and underwriting decision.

What Short-Term Medical Insurance Is

Short-term, limited-duration insurance (STLDI) is designed primarily for temporary transitions between other forms of coverage. For policies sold or issued on or after September 1, 2024, federal rules limit the initial contract term to no more than three months and the total coverage period—including renewals or extensions by the same issuer group—to no more than four months.

Those federal limits do not guarantee that a policy is available in every state, county, or situation. Florida availability, underwriting rules, networks, and policy forms must be confirmed at the time of application.

Benefits may include

  • Covered emergency, inpatient hospital, surgery, urgent care, diagnostic, or physician services.
  • A provider network or negotiated-rate arrangement defined by the policy.
  • Choice among deductibles, coinsurance, and maximum-benefit structures offered by the issuer.

Common limitations to investigate

  • Pre-existing conditions: prior symptoms, diagnoses, treatment, or medication history may affect eligibility or claims.
  • Medical underwriting: an application may be declined or issued with exclusions.
  • Prescription drugs: outpatient drug coverage may be limited or absent.
  • Maternity, mental health, preventive, and habilitative care: benefits may differ materially from ACA-compliant coverage.
  • Benefit maximums: a policy may cap payments by service, condition, term, or total benefit.
  • Renewal: coverage is temporary and should not be assumed renewable beyond the permitted term.

When It May Be Considered

A short-term policy may be worth evaluating when a documented coverage gap is genuinely brief and comprehensive options have already been checked. Examples can include a waiting period before new employer coverage begins or a transition with a known near-term end date.

Evaluate these conditions first

  • The gap fits within the policy’s permitted effective and termination dates.
  • You have reviewed ACA Special Enrollment eligibility, COBRA or state continuation, a spouse’s employer plan, Medicaid or CHIP eligibility, and other comprehensive options.
  • Your doctors, hospitals, prescriptions, and expected services have been checked against the actual policy—not a marketing summary.
  • You can absorb the deductible, coinsurance, non-covered services, and any amount above the policy’s benefit maximum.

When It Is Usually a Poor Fit

  • You manage an ongoing condition or take regular prescriptions.
  • You anticipate pregnancy, surgery, therapy, specialist treatment, or recurring diagnostics.
  • You need reliable preventive, behavioral health, maternity, rehabilitation, or prescription coverage.
  • Your preferred providers or facilities cannot be verified as participating.
  • The coverage gap could continue past the policy’s termination date.
  • You would be unable to pay the deductible and uncovered charges if a claim occurs.

Short-Term Coverage Compared With ACA Coverage

Short-term medical

  • Temporary term with federal and state limits.
  • May use medical underwriting.
  • May exclude pre-existing conditions.
  • Benefits and maximums vary by policy.
  • Not eligible for ACA premium tax credits.

ACA-compliant major medical

  • Guaranteed issue during an eligible enrollment period.
  • Covers essential health benefits under applicable ACA rules.
  • Does not exclude pre-existing conditions.
  • Includes an annual in-network out-of-pocket maximum.
  • May qualify for income-based premium tax credits.

How to Review a Short-Term Policy

1. Start with the policy documents

Review the outline of coverage, certificate, exclusions, limitations, schedule of benefits, definition of a pre-existing condition, and maximum-benefit provisions. A quote screen is not the policy.

2. Confirm dates and replacement coverage

Know the requested effective date, termination date, cancellation rules, and the exact date comprehensive replacement coverage is expected to begin. Do not assume another short-term policy will be available afterward.

3. Verify providers and prescriptions

Check the issuer’s current directory and contact both the provider and issuer. Confirm hospitals, physicians, laboratories, imaging facilities, and prescriptions separately. Network participation does not guarantee that a service is covered.

4. Model the full financial exposure

Add the premium, deductible, coinsurance, non-covered services, out-of-network exposure, and any amount above a benefit limit. Compare that total with comprehensive alternatives—not just the monthly premium.

5. Answer underwriting questions completely

Provide accurate application information. Omissions can affect eligibility, policy rescission rights, or claims depending on the policy and applicable law.

Comprehensive Alternatives to Check First

ACA Special Enrollment

Loss of qualifying coverage may create a Special Enrollment Period. HealthCare.gov states that many applicants can enroll during the 60 days before or after a qualifying loss; documentation and effective-date rules apply.

COBRA or state continuation

Continuation coverage can preserve the employer plan’s benefit structure and accumulated deductible status. Compare the full premium, election deadline, retroactive-election rules, and duration.

Other household or public coverage

Check eligibility for a spouse’s employer plan, a parent’s plan when applicable, Medicaid, CHIP, Medicare, or another comprehensive program before relying on temporary underwritten coverage.

Questions to Ask Before Applying

  1. What services and conditions are excluded?
  2. How does the policy define a pre-existing condition?
  3. What are the deductible, coinsurance, and maximum-benefit limits?
  4. Which providers and facilities participate, and how is out-of-network care handled?
  5. How are prescriptions, mental health, maternity, preventive care, and follow-up treatment covered?
  6. What is the exact termination date, and what replacement coverage will begin next?
  7. What appeal, cancellation, and claims-review rights apply?

Source-backed review

See the CMS short-term insurance fact sheet and HealthCare.gov Special Enrollment guidance. Policy terms, current availability, and eligibility still require quote-level verification.
