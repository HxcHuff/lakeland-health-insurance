Do I have to pay back the premium tax credit?

If you used advance premium tax credits, you must reconcile them when you file your federal income tax return. You do not automatically repay the full credit. You compare the amount paid in advance with the amount you qualify for based on final household information.

By David Huff, licensed Florida health agent #W371813 | NPN 18213932

Last reviewed: September 29, 2026.

Direct answer: You reconcile, which is not the same as always writing a check for the full credit. The IRS and HealthCare.gov explain that Form 8962 compares advance payments with the premium tax credit you qualify for. If advance payments were higher, you may repay some or all of the difference. If they were lower, you may claim the rest as a credit. For tax years after 2025, the IRS says there is no repayment cap.

What does reconciling actually mean?

The Marketplace sends Form 1095-A. You use it with IRS reconciliation instructions and HealthCare.gov tax reconciling guidance. The return then calculates the allowed credit from final MAGI, household size, and benchmark premium rules for that year.

This page is the yes-or-no repayment question. For wrong estimates entered at signup, see ACA subsidy clawback when income is reported wrong. For updating a current policy, see income changes after you enroll.

When would I not repay anything extra?

If the advance payments match the final credit, reconciliation can be zero. If you used less than you qualified for, the difference can increase a refund or reduce tax owed. If you used more, you repay the excess on your return. The IRS says that for tax years after 2025 there is no repayment cap, so the full excess is added to your balance due or subtracted from your refund. Caps applied only to earlier tax years.

What if I received 1095-A forms that look wrong?

Correct Marketplace data before you file when you can. HealthCare.gov explains how to request a corrected 1095-A. Filing with an incorrect form can create a repayment that does not match the coverage you actually had. Keep monthly premium statements and eligibility notices.

Does using an agent change the tax result?

No. Agents do not set the credit. The Marketplace and the IRS do. David Huff, a licensed Florida health agent, can help you review whether the application still matches your income. A tax professional should prepare Form 8962. See using an agent for HealthCare.gov.

What if I had Marketplace coverage for only part of the year?

Reconciliation is monthly. Form 1095-A shows coverage months. If you had employer coverage or Medicare for part of the year, the credit calculation should match those months. Filing as if you had a full year of Marketplace coverage when you did not can create a repayment that does not match the facts.

If two people in a household had different coverage months, the form still follows the tax household. That is another reason to give the tax preparer the 1095-A and the employer coverage dates together. See what counts as income if MAGI is the disputed piece.

David Huff can help you request a corrected 1095-A through the Marketplace process. He cannot file Form 8962. Use a qualified tax professional for the return.

What documents should I give a tax preparer?

Bring Form 1095-A, the Marketplace eligibility notices, and a note of any coverage months that were not Marketplace months. If you married, divorced, or added a dependent, say so. Repayment and additional credit both follow the tax household, not only the person whose name is on the insurance card.

If you received more than one 1095-A because you changed plans, give every form. Do not pick the one with the friendlier numbers. See HealthCare.gov on reconciling the premium tax credit.
